Money Foundations

Start with a clear picture of what comes in and what goes out

CFPB financial-education tools use income tracking, spending tracking, bill calendars, and cash-flow budgets to help people understand their money patterns before making larger decisions.

1. LearnIncome, expenses & cash flow
2. PracticeBuild a monthly snapshot
3. CheckTake a mini-quiz
4. ReflectStewardship & attention

Part 1 · Learn

Three ideas to understand first

Take-home income

The money available after payroll deductions or other withholdings. Use one consistent monthly amount in the practice tool.

Expenses & obligations

Money going out for housing, utilities, food, transportation, debt, subscriptions, giving, savings goals, and other spending.

Cash-flow remainder

Income minus the outflow you modeled. It is a snapshot that can help you notice patterns—not a judgment of your worth.

Needs, wants, and obligations vary by household

Some expenses are essential or required; others are more flexible. The useful first step is seeing where money goes before deciding what, if anything, you want to change.

Saving & Emergency Funds

Build a cushion for the unexpected without turning a target into a rule

CFPB describes an emergency fund as money set aside for unplanned expenses or financial emergencies and notes that the amount someone needs depends on their situation. Even small amounts can help provide a buffer.

1. LearnPurpose of emergency savings
2. PracticeModel a savings goal
3. CheckTest the concepts
4. ReflectPatience & consistency

Part 1 · Learn

Emergency savings is for financial shocks

Unplanned expense

Examples can include car repairs, home repairs, medical bills, or a loss of income.

Personal target

There is no single amount this app tells everyone to save. A useful target depends on your situation, expenses, risks, and other goals.

Accessible & separate

CFPB suggests thinking about a place that is safe, accessible, and not too tempting to use for non-emergencies.

Using emergency savings is not “failing”

If an unexpected expense fits the purpose you set for the fund, using it is what the fund is for. Afterward, you can work on rebuilding it over time.

03

Debt & Credit

Know the difference between a credit report, a credit score, and debt-to-income math

A credit report describes credit activity and account history. A credit score is a prediction based on credit-report information using a scoring model. CFPB also defines debt-to-income ratio (DTI) as monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income.

1. LearnReports, scores & DTI
2. PracticeCalculate DTI
3. CheckCredit knowledge quiz
4. ReflectTruth over shame

Part 1 · Learn

Three terms that are easy to mix up

Credit report

A record containing information about your credit activity and current credit situation.

Credit score

A score calculated from credit-report information. Different data, models, products, and timing can produce different scores.

DTI

Monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. Lenders may use it as one measure, but requirements differ.

Reviewing reports matters

CFPB recommends reviewing credit reports for mistakes. The federally authorized site for free credit reports is AnnualCreditReport.com. This project is not affiliated with that site.

Home Buying & Equity

Learn the pieces without turning an estimate into an approval

Early home-buying education can include down payment, closing costs, emergency savings, ongoing housing costs, credit, and comparison shopping. CFPB says closing costs typically range from about 2% to 5% of the purchase price, excluding the down payment, while actual costs vary.

1. LearnUpfront costs & equity
2. PracticeRun rough illustrations
3. CheckHome-buying quiz
4. ReflectHome without idolatry

Part 1 · Learn

Preparation has more than one number

Down payment

The portion of the purchase price paid upfront rather than financed. Requirements vary by loan and situation.

Closing costs

Upfront transaction and loan costs separate from the down payment. CFPB gives 2%–5% as a rough early planning range.

Home equity

Generally, current property value minus the amount owed on existing mortgage debt. It is not the same as cash in hand.

Investing Basics

Learn risk, diversification, fees, and compounding before chasing returns

Investor.gov explains that investing involves risk, including the possibility of losing money. Diversification can reduce concentration risk but cannot eliminate investment losses.

1. LearnProducts, risk & diversification
2. PracticeHypothetical growth math
3. CheckInvesting quiz
4. ReflectPatience over greed

Part 1 · Learn

Foundational investing vocabulary

Stocks

Shares of ownership in a company. Prices can rise or fall and investors can lose money.

Bonds

Debt obligations in which an investor lends money to an issuer under stated terms.

Funds

Products such as mutual funds and ETFs can hold collections of investments. Features, risks, fees, and diversification vary.

Risk and return are connected

Higher potential return generally comes with greater risk. Your goals, time horizon, risk tolerance, product details, fees, liquidity, and fraud risk can all matter. This project does not recommend any security or allocation.

Scams & Financial Red Flags

Slow down when someone wants you to rush

FTC guidance identifies guaranteed or unusually large returns, claims of little or no risk, vague details, “secret” systems, and pressure to act quickly as common investment-scam warning signs.

1. LearnRecognize common red flags
2. PracticeWork through scenarios
3. CheckFraud-awareness quiz
4. ReflectPeace over pressure

Part 1 · Learn

Four reasons to stop and verify

Guaranteed profit

Promises of guaranteed high returns or risk-free investing are major warning signs.

Urgency

Pressure to act immediately can be designed to keep you from researching.

Secrecy

“Secret methods” and “proven systems” are not substitutes for transparent information.

Unverified people or firms

Research who is making the offer and verify registrations or licenses when applicable.

Verify before you trust.

For investment professionals and firms, Investor.gov provides tools for checking registration and background information. For suspected scams, FTC guidance explains how to report fraud.

Visit Investor.govFTC ReportFraud

Part 2 · Practice

Red-flag scenarios

Scenario A

“Guaranteed 20% return. No risk. Offer ends tonight.”

Scenario B

Someone you met online moves the conversation to a private messaging app and offers to teach you a crypto strategy.

Scenario C

A promoter says, “Don't research this online. Negative reviews are from people who don't understand the system.”

Pressure is not the same thing as wisdom
